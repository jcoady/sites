Section 01 · Projective Metrical Foundations

Points, lines, and pole–polar duality replace distance and angle

In three-dimensional relativistic space equipped with the symmetric bilinear form \([x_1, y_1, z_1] \cdot [x_2, y_2, z_2] = x_1x_2 + y_1y_2 - z_1z_2\), a projective point \(a = [x : y : z]\) is a line through the origin, and a projective line \(L = (l : m : n)\) is a plane through the origin with incidence \(lx + my - nz = 0\). Intersecting with the plane \(z = 1\) maps \(a\) to the affine point \((X, Y) = (x/z, y/z)\) and turns the null cone \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0\) into the blue null circle \(X^2 + Y^2 = 1\).

Unlike classical hyperbolic models that discard points outside the unit disk, UHG treats interior points (hyperboloid of two sheets), exterior points (hyperboloid of one sheet), and null points on the circle symmetrically. Because the metric form is symmetric, every point \(a = [x : y : z]\) corresponds canonically to its dual polar line \(a^\perp = (x : y : z)\), and every line \(L = (l : m : n)\) to its dual pole \(L^\perp = [l : m : n]\).

DefinitionProjective Quadrance & Spread

For non-null points \(a_1, a_2\) and non-null lines \(L_1, L_2\), separation is measured by two dual rational invariants:

\[q(a_1, a_2) = 1 - \frac{(a_1 \cdot a_2)^2}{(a_1 \cdot a_1)(a_2 \cdot a_2)}, \qquad S(L_1, L_2) = 1 - \frac{(L_1 \cdot L_2)^2}{(L_1 \cdot L_1)(L_2 \cdot L_2)}\]

By Quadrance–Spread Duality, \(q(a_1, a_2) = S(a_1^\perp, a_2^\perp)\). Points or lines are perpendicular precisely when this value equals \(1\). Inside the disk, \(q = -\sinh^2(d)\) and \(S = \sin^2(\theta)\).

Section 02 · Null Quadrangle Geometry

Any four null points induce a self-polar, fully-right diagonal triangle

Null points \(\alpha = [x : y : z]\) satisfying \(x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 0\) can be parametrized rationally without trigonometry using Pythagorean triples: \(\alpha(t : u) = [t^2 - u^2 : 2tu : t^2 + u^2]\). Choose four distinct null points \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \alpha_4\) around the null circle. Their six connecting lines intersect in three diagonal points: \[e = (\alpha_1\alpha_2)(\alpha_3\alpha_4), \qquad f = (\alpha_1\alpha_3)(\alpha_2\alpha_4), \qquad g = (\alpha_1\alpha_4)(\alpha_2\alpha_3)\]

A foundational theorem of projective conics—and the starting point of Coady’s construction—asserts that \(efg\) is simultaneously self-polar and fully-right: each vertex is the dual pole of its opposite side, and every pair of diagonal points or diagonal lines is mutually perpendicular.

Theorem 1Nil Quadrangle Diagonal & The 48/64 Constants

For distinct null points \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3, \alpha_4\) with diagonal points \(e, f, g\):

\[e^\perp = fg, \quad f^\perp = ge, \quad g^\perp = ef \implies q(e,f) = q(f,g) = q(g,e) = 1\]

Moreover, Wildberger’s 48/64 Theorem shows that the three diagonal spreads \(P = S(\alpha_1\alpha_2, \alpha_3\alpha_4)\), \(R = S(\alpha_1\alpha_3, \alpha_2\alpha_4)\), and \(T = S(\alpha_1\alpha_4, \alpha_2\alpha_3)\) satisfy two universal constants independent of the quadrangle: \[PR + RT + TP = 48, \qquad PRT = 64, \qquad \frac{1}{P} + \frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{T} = \frac{3}{4}\]

Section 03 · Coady’s Reciprocal Parallax Theorem

Projecting one null vertex onto perpendicular diagonal legs locks q₂₃ · q₃₄ = 1

Relabel the unique interior diagonal point \(e\) as \(a_3\), and pick one of the original null vertices, \(a_1 = \alpha_4\). From the two exterior diagonal poles \(f\) and \(g\), project \(a_1\) onto the perpendicular diagonal legs \(a_3g\) and \(a_3f\): \[a_2 \equiv (fa_1) \cdot (a_3g), \qquad a_4 \equiv (ga_1) \cdot (a_3f)\] Step through the four-stage proof below to see how polarity, complementary spreads, and the Right Parallax Theorem force \(q_{23}\) and \(q_{34}\) to become exact reciprocals.

Section 04 · The Hyperbola xy = 1 & Fixed-Point Involution

Rational parallax parametrizes xy = 1 around the self-dual point q = −1

Treating the spread \(S = S_2\) as a rational coordinate on the projective line, the complementary quadrances \((x, y) = (q_{23}, q_{34}) = \left(\frac{S-1}{S},\; \frac{S}{S-1}\right)\) trace the standard hyperbola \(xy = 1\) for all \(S \neq 0, 1\). Remarkably, although the construction begins within the “red” geometry of null points and perpendicularity, it produces the exact defining equation of the “green” unit circle in Wildberger’s chromogeometry—mirroring Lobachevsky and Bolyai’s transcendental angle-of-parallelism relation \(\tan(\theta/2) = e^{-d}\) in purely algebraic form.

Because \(q \mapsto 1/q\) is a projective cross-ratio involution, its algebraic fixed points satisfy \(q^2 = 1\), or \(q = \pm 1\). Yet \(q = +1\) is geometrically impossible here: substituting \(q = 1\) into \(q = (S - 1)/S\) yields \(S = S - 1\), a contradiction. Thus \(q = -1\) at \(S = 1/2\) is the unique realizable self-dual fixed point, where both parallax triangles share \(q_{23} = q_{34} = -1\) and \(S_2 = S_4 = 1/2\).

Section 05 · Foundational Algebraic Laws

Five polynomial laws unify affine, hyperbolic, and elliptic geometry

As Wildberger shows in Affine and Projective Universal Geometry, the fundamental divide in metrical geometry is not between Euclidean and non-Euclidean spaces, but between affine trigonometry (on vectors) and projective trigonometry (on lines through the origin). The projective laws act as universal polynomial deformations of the affine ones—and hold verbatim in both hyperbolic (\(x^2+y^2-z^2\)) and elliptic (\(x^2+y^2+z^2\)) geometries, over \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\mathbb{R}\), and even finite fields \(\mathbb{F}_p\).

Higher ExtensionsFrom Triangles to Quadrangles and Projective Tetrahedra

The algebraic machinery behind Coady’s reciprocal parallax theorem extends directly to higher configurations. Using Archimedes’ function \(A(x, y, z) = (x+y+z)^2 - 2(x^2+y^2+z^2)\), Coady derived exact rational formulas for the quadrea \(B(a_1a_2a_3a_4)\) of a projective quadrangle and its dual quadreal over \(\mathbb{Q}\)—reducing to a sum of adjacent triangle quadreas when a vertex spread is \(1\)—and generalized the framework to 3-dimensional projective tetrahedra via the projective quadrume \(V = A_k h_k\) and vertex solid spreads.
